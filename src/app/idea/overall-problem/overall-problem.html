Overall problem

LLM knowledge is restricted to the public information available at the moment of training. If a company wants the model to work effectively with private data, it has to somehow show and explain this data to the model. One way to do that is RAG - retrieval-augmented generation - which searches the documents first, hands the best pieces to the model, and lets it write the answer from them.

There is a problem with RAG when it comes to multi-hop questions, which are questions that need facts from two or more documents. Take the question "Where was the director of Inception born?" The search easily finds the document about the film, because it shares a lot of words with the question, and from that document we learn that the director is Christopher Nolan. The fact we actually need, his birthplace, is in a different document about Nolan himself. That document never mentions Inception, so it shares neither the key words nor the meaning of the question. Hybrid search, which combines keyword search with meaning-based search and then reranks the results, therefore scores it low and can easily miss it.

GraphRAG tries to solve this by building a graph of the people, places and other things mentioned in the documents, together with the links between them. Once the search finds the film, it can follow the link from the film to its director and from the director to his birthplace, and in this way it reaches the second document. The downside is cost. A language model has to read every document to build the graph, and it has to read the changed ones again whenever the collection is updated. On top of that, studies show that whether the graph actually helps depends on the data and on the type of question.

KET-RAG reduces this cost by building the graph for only part of the documents. It picks the pieces of text that are most central, meaning the ones most strongly connected to other similar pieces. However, being central does not guarantee that a piece will be useful for answering questions. My research looks for properties of a piece of text that can be measured before any links are extracted from it and that predict whether those links will be needed for questions nobody has asked yet.
