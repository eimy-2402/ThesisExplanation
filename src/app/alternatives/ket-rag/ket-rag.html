KET-RAG

KET-RAG builds the expensive graph for only part of the collection, and this is the method I plan to improve.

First, KET-RAG chooses the “core” chunks. To do this, it builds a similarity graph in which each chunk is linked to the chunks most similar to it. Half of these neighbours are found through shared keywords and the other half through similar meaning, which is measured with text embeddings. It then ranks every chunk with PageRank, a centrality score under which a chunk ranks high when other high-ranking chunks link to it. The chunks with the highest scores become the core, and the share of chunks that goes into the core, called β, is fixed in advance.

Only the core chunks are sent to the LLM, which extracts entities and relations from them in the same way as GraphRAG. The result is called a “knowledge graph skeleton”. In addition, KET-RAG builds a cheap keyword graph for all chunks, in which each chunk is linked to the words it contains, except stop words. This graph does not need an LLM at all. When a question arrives, part of the context comes from local search on the skeleton and part comes from chunks linked to keywords that are similar to the question, and the two parts are then joined together.

This makes indexing cheaper, because the LLM reads fewer chunks, while the keyword graph still covers the whole collection. By default, 80% of the chunks are core, which means β = 0.8. The authors report about 20% lower indexing cost, together with answers that are up to 32.4% better.

However, centrality only shows that a chunk is well connected to other similar chunks. It does not show whether questions will actually need the links extracted from that chunk. For this reason, I plan to keep the overall KET-RAG approach but change the rule that decides which chunks get a graph, as the table below shows.
